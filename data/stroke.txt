Stroke

Stroke is a medical emergency that occurs when blood flow to the brain is interrupted. This may happen because a blood vessel becomes blocked or because a blood vessel in the brain ruptures and causes bleeding. Without enough blood and oxygen, brain cells begin to die within a short period, making immediate medical treatment essential.

Types of Stroke

There are three main types of stroke:

1. Ischemic Stroke
This is the most common type of stroke. It occurs when a blood clot blocks a blood vessel supplying the brain, reducing or stopping blood flow.

2. Hemorrhagic Stroke
This type occurs when a blood vessel in the brain ruptures, causing bleeding into the surrounding brain tissue.

3. Transient Ischemic Attack (TIA)
A transient ischemic attack is a temporary blockage of blood flow to the brain. Symptoms are similar to a stroke but usually disappear within a few minutes and do not cause permanent brain damage. A TIA is an important warning sign of future stroke risk.

Risk Factors

Some stroke risk factors can be modified, while others cannot.

Modifiable risk factors include:
- High blood pressure
- Tobacco use
- High cholesterol
- Diabetes
- Overweight or obesity
- Physical inactivity
- Unhealthy diet
- Excessive alcohol use
- Air pollution
- Kidney dysfunction

Non-modifiable risk factors include:
- Older age
- Previous stroke
- Heart diseases such as atrial fibrillation or heart failure
- Chronic kidney disease

Warning Signs and Symptoms

Common signs of stroke include:
- Sudden loss of balance or coordination
- Sudden vision problems
- Facial drooping
- Weakness or numbness in one arm or both arms
- Difficulty speaking or slurred speech

Anyone experiencing these symptoms should receive emergency medical care immediately.

Diagnosis and Treatment

Stroke is a medical emergency that requires rapid diagnosis.

Healthcare professionals may use CT scans or MRI scans to determine the type of stroke.

Treatment depends on the type of stroke:
- Ischemic stroke may be treated with clot-dissolving medications or mechanical clot removal.
- Hemorrhagic stroke may require blood pressure control, intensive monitoring, or surgery.

Early treatment and specialized stroke care improve recovery and reduce complications.

Prevention

The risk of stroke can be reduced by adopting healthy lifestyle habits, including:
- Controlling high blood pressure
- Stopping tobacco use
- Eating a balanced diet rich in fruits and vegetables
- Reducing salt, sugar, and unhealthy fats
- Exercising regularly
- Limiting alcohol consumption
- Managing diabetes and cholesterol
- Improving air quality whenever possible

People at high risk may also require medications such as antiplatelet agents, anticoagulants, or cholesterol-lowering drugs under medical supervision.

Complications

Stroke may cause short-term and long-term complications.

Possible complications include:
- Brain swelling
- Speech and language problems
- Difficulty swallowing
- Seizures
- Depression
- Blood clots
- Muscle stiffness
- Walking difficulties
- Memory and cognitive impairment
- Chronic pain
- Loss of independence
- Urinary incontinence

Rehabilitation

Stroke rehabilitation should begin as soon as the patient's condition becomes stable.

Rehabilitation may involve:
- Physical therapy
- Occupational therapy
- Speech and language therapy
- Cognitive rehabilitation
- Psychological support and counseling

Early rehabilitation improves recovery, independence, and quality of life.

WHO Response

The World Health Organization (WHO) supports countries in improving stroke prevention, diagnosis, treatment, rehabilitation, and long-term care. WHO also promotes hypertension control, healthy lifestyles, and evidence-based stroke management through global initiatives and clinical guidelines.
